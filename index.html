Where is the population mean?

You cannot see the true population mean. Take one random sample, then work through the calculations to construct a confidence interval that estimates where it might be.

1. Unknown population

The population follows a normal distribution. Its numerical scale and true mean are hidden. The graphs below use the same horizontal scale, so you can compare your sample with the population.

2

Calculate the estimated standard error

The sample standard deviation estimates the population spread. Divide by the square root of your sample size.

SE = s / √n
3

Look up the critical value in the t-table

The margin of error needs a critical value, t*. First find your degrees of freedom, then use that row and your confidence level's column in the table. If your df is not listed, use the next smaller df in the table.

5

Find the two endpoints

Subtract the margin of error from the sample mean to find the lower endpoint; add it to find the upper endpoint. After both endpoints are correct, the confidence interval will appear on the graph.

Lower = x̄ − margin of error   |   Upper = x̄ + margin of error

You've constructed a confidence interval from one sample. The confidence level describes how often this method captures the true mean in repeated random sampling; it does not guarantee that this particular interval contains it.

The population is normal, observations are independent, and the interval uses the sample standard deviation and a t-critical value read from the t-table. Answers within 0.03 of the calculated results are accepted; round to two decimals.
